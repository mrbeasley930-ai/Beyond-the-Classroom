The CaveSix stages, from the shadows on the wall to the sun, and back down to tell the others

Free things for sixth-form Classical Civilisation students: lectures, podcasts, the texts in full, tools for looking at the objects themselves, essay prizes, and the way into a Classics degree. Most of them are tied to what you study: the Odyssey and the Aeneid, Love and Relationships, the Invention of the Barbarian and Greek Art. The page is the cave in Plato's Republic, and you start chained in it, watching shadows. Six stages, each one kind of thing: the Wall (watch), the Fire (listen), the Steep Way (read), Outside (do), the Sun (enter), the Descent (apply). Every item is a stone on the way up; do it and the page gets lighter. The Mouth of the Cave brings in classics news every morning. Glaux, Athena's owl, can see in the dark and will show you round. Everything saves to the department.

Classics in the news

Collected automatically every morning: new pieces from Antigone (an open online journal of classics written for anyone), Sententiae Antiquae (a passage of Greek or Latin with a translation, most days), Archaeology magazine, the Greek Archaeology Newsroom, the Guardian's archaeology and classics desks, The Conversation and Phys.org (Greek and Roman stories only); and new episodes of Natalie Haynes Stands Up for the Classics, In Our Time (classical episodes only), The Rest Is History (ancient episodes only), The Ancients and The Partial Historians. Read or listen to one, then press Take it in and write two sentences: what it was, and which part of the course it touches (a text, an object, an idea, a scholar). It saves to the department and goes on your tablet. Each item is tagged to the part of the course it seems to belong to; the tags come from keywords, so some will be wrong, and finding the better link is part of the exercise.

Your tablet

Greeks and Romans wrote their notes on wax tablets with a stylus. Write a line for anything you watch, listen to, read, do or enter: what it was, its point in one line, what you found out, and a question it left you with. Classics applicants are asked what they have read beyond the course and what they made of it. This is where your answer comes from, and where your personal statement and your teacher's reference start.
