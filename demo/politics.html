The Division Lobby · Westminster

The Division · the House's most recent recorded votes

Vote before you see the result

These are the most recent divisions in the House of Commons, straight from Parliament's own record. On each one, decide how you would have voted, Aye or No, and write your reason: at least fifteen words, and it has to be an argument, not a feeling. Only then does the page show you how the House divided and how each party split. Read the title carefully: a reasoned amendment is a vote to refuse a bill a second reading, so Aye there means no to the bill. Your votes and reasons save to the department and go into your Hansard. The bell gives you eight minutes, as it does Members; here the doors do not lock.

The Order Paper · new every morning

Politics in the news

Collected automatically every morning from the BBC's politics desk, the Guardian, The Conversation, the House of Commons Library, UCL's Constitution Unit, the Institute for Government, UK in a Changing Europe, the LSE and the Electoral Reform Society; from Washington, NPR, the Guardian's US desk and SCOTUSblog; and new episodes of the politics podcasts, most of them on the department's own list: Parliament Matters, The News Agents (UK and USA), The Rest Is Politics, the Spectator's Coffee House Shots, the Guardian's Politics Weekly, the FT's Political Fix, Westminster Insider, the IfG's Inside Briefing, the NPR Politics Podcast and The Last Best Hope?. Read or listen to one, then press Log it and write two sentences: what happened, and which part of the course it touches (an institution, a process, a concept, a thinker). It saves to the department and goes into your Hansard. Each item is tagged to the paper it seems to belong to. The tags come from keywords, so some will be wrong; finding a better link is part of the exercise. Every source has a view; the page says what each one is.

Write an entry for anything you listen to, watch, read, do, enter or vote on: what it was, its point in one line, what you found out, and a question it left you with. Politics applicants are asked what they have read beyond the course and what they made of it. What the department would see

A separate teacher page, with no setup beyond pasting a token once: every pupil who has signed in; who tapped I want to enter, Put my name down or I'm interested (your list for the Parliament visit, Learn with the Lords, Model UN and the Youth Parliament, and a heads-up on who is entering the R.A. Butler, FT-APG and Orwell prizes or applying for UNIQ on their own); how much of the half-term paper each pupil has done; each pupil's items by room and by paper (P1, P2, P3), so you can see who never touches the US or the ideas; every division each pupil has voted in, Aye or No, with their reason and whether they were with the majority; every article and podcast logged from the Order Paper with the two sentences; each pupil's Hansard in full, as copyable text for references and personal statements; who on your form list has not signed in yet; a CSV. The half-term paper is edited in one block at the top of the file; the Order Paper and the Division update themselves; pupils keep everything they have done.

Anything on this page can be changed for Politics: the items, the paper tags, which sources the Order Paper reads and which podcasts, whether the Division also takes Lords divisions or US roll-call votes, the dates, the Doorkeeper's lines, what gets tracked, the look of it.
